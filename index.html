How a flight goes

Aim, glide, land, fold.

Three things happen every throw, and the third is the one that compounds.

1.

Draw back and let go

Drag anywhere to set the angle and the power. A drag that is too short, or one that points forward, isn't a throw — the plane leaves your hand only when you mean it.

2.

Stay up longer than you should

Flap for height, ride an updraft, hit a firecracker for speed. Skim within four metres of a roof and every coin you take is worth up to five times as much — right up until you climb and lose it.

3.

Land, then fold something in

Between throws the game turns into a blueprint. Spend what you caught on any of fifteen tracks, walk back out to the rooftop, and throw the same sheet of paper again — a little better folded.

The workshop

Fifteen things to fold in.

Thrust and hull are the obvious ones. The rest are stranger: a magnet that widens what counts as catching a coin, a storm coat that stops a cloud from eating your speed, wax that keeps you sliding after you land.

Coins and levels reset in every new city. A visit is a build you commit to, not a checklist you tick off — and the plane you fold in Tokyo is not the one you'll want in Cairo.

The tour

It starts famous, then gets stranger.

The first cities are the ones on everybody's postcard. Keep going and the names stop being ones you already know — and no two stops in a row sit on the same continent, so the map never settles into a straight line.

Each city hides its own artifacts: one high, one mid, one you only reach by skimming a roof. Those you keep. They outlive the reset, fill a page in the atlas, and are the only thing you carry from one city to the next.

What a city costs you

Every city takes the coins back and hands you a blank sheet. What it can't take is the distance you already flew, the artifacts you already found, or the fact that you now know what a thermal feels like.

A city ends when you cross its flag in a single flight. There is no way to lose one — only to throw again.

Drawn, not photographed

Every building is a line, not a picture.

There are no image files in Paper Skyline. The gulls, the shopfronts, the fog rolling in off the bay, the fold lines on the plane itself — all of it is drawing code, worked out in numbers and redrawn every frame.

Which means the game downloads in about the space of a photograph, stays sharp on any screen you put it on, and looks hand-drawn because, in the only sense that matters, it is.

The art on this page is the same idea: every plane, coin and rooftop you have scrolled past is written into the page as shapes and numbers. No pictures were loaded to draw any of it.

Plainly

Your progress stays on your phone

No account, no sign-in, nothing to create. The game writes your run, your charted cities and your artifacts to the device and reads them back. Deleting the app deletes all of it. The full policy is short.

Landscape, one thumb

Every control is a drag or a tap on the same screen you are already watching. No buttons to hunt for, no second hand needed.

Nothing to lose

There is no fail state and no timer. A throw that goes badly costs you the throw. You pick the plane up and throw it again, as many times as a city takes.
